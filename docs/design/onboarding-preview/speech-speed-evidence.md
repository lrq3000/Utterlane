# Evidence for the Everyday Uses comparison

Reviewed 2026-10-05. This comparison is about human speech and smartphone typing,
not speech-recognition throughput or a measured productivity gain in Utterlane.

## Mobile typing: 36.2 WPM

Palin, K., Feit, A. M., Kim, S., Kristensson, P. O., and Oulasvirta, A. (2019).
**How do People Type on Mobile Devices? Observations from a Study with 37,000
Volunteers.** ACM MobileHCI 2019.

- [Authors' project and publication page](https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/typing37k/)
- [Author-hosted paper](https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/typing37k/resources/Mobile_typing_study.pdf)
- Inspected the abstract, collection procedure, metrics, and limitations in the
  paper, in addition to the authors' project page.
- 37,370 volunteers copied 15 English sentences on mobile devices using their
  preferred keyboards. The mean was **36.2 WPM**, with **2.3% uncorrected errors**.
- A typing “word” is the conventional **five characters**. Time runs between the
  first and last keystroke. This is copied text, not spontaneous composition.
- Self-selected typing-test visitors, many young US women, are not a
  representative sample of all smartphone users. Data were collected in
  September 2018–January 2019; this is not a current-device benchmark.

## Natural conversation: 164 WPM under the turn-wise definition

Yuan, J., Liberman, M., and Cieri, C. (2006). **Towards an Integrated
Understanding of Speaking Rate in Conversation.** Interspeech 2006, 541–544.
DOI: **10.21437/Interspeech.2006-204**.

- [Conference record](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/yuan06_interspeech.html)
- [Published paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/yuan06_interspeech.pdf)
- Inspected all four pages. Section 2, page 542, examines 2,438 English
  Switchboard conversations. These are natural telephone conversations, **not
  dictation, voice typing, or ASR performance tests**.
- **164 WPM**: total words divided by the summed time of each speaker's turns.
  Published segment boundaries can overlap; this is a measure with important
  segmentation limitations, not a universal average for every speaker.
- The same recordings produce **196 WPM** when both participants' words are
  divided by total conversation elapsed time, and **236 WPM** when silence and
  non-speech noises are excluded. These definitions must not be interchanged.
- Use the lower, turn-wise figure in the comparison and label it explicitly in
  the source details. Do not imply the 196 figure measures a single person's
  rate independently of the other participant.

## Cross-checks and limits

Google Scholar was used to discover and cross-check the literature. Lee and
Doherty (2017), *Speaking rate and articulation rate of native speakers of Irish
English*, DOI **10.1080/2050571X.2017.1290337**, was also inspected in its
[accepted manuscript](https://hdl.handle.net/10468/6744). Its 44 participants
produced spontaneous face-to-face speech; it reports **syllables**, not words,
per minute. It supports variation by task and dialect but cannot be substituted
into a words-per-minute ratio without extra assumptions. The publisher full text
of Tauroza and Allison (1990) was inaccessible, so its numerical findings are not
used as independently verified evidence here.

These are separate studies with different tasks, populations, and word-count
definitions. **164 / 36.2 = approximately 4.5** is an illustrative cross-study
comparison, not a controlled effect size, an estimate for all 25 supported
languages, or a prediction of an individual's time saving. There is no support
here for “you speak 20× faster” or “Utterlane writes as fast and accurately”.
Utterlane speed and accuracy depend on model, device, audio, and correction time.
